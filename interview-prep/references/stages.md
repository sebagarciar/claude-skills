# Stage playbooks

Every stage is a different assessment run by a different person with a different fear. Prepare
for the fear, not the job title.

Not every process has every stage. Build the brief around the stages the research actually
supports, and say which ones are inferred.

---

## Recruiter screen (25 to 45 minutes)

**Who:** a recruiter or sourcer. Usually not a domain expert.

**Their fear:** wasting the hiring manager's time, and losing a candidate late over money,
location or timing.

**What they are really testing:** can you explain your background in two minutes without
rambling, are the logistics workable, is the motivation real, and does the story on the CV hold
together out loud.

**Nearly always asked:** walk me through your background. Why us. Why this role. What are you
looking for. Salary expectations. Notice period and availability. Work authorisation. Location
and office days. Are you in other processes.

**For Seba specifically:** the MBA finishing December 2026 and the EU work permit both come up
here, every time. Both need a clean, unhesitant, ten-second answer. Prepare them.

**The trap:** treating this as a formality. Recruiters cut more candidates than anyone else in
the process.

**Time split:** two minutes on background, then short answers. Do not case-solve here.

---

## Hiring manager (45 to 60 minutes)

**Who:** the person who would manage him.

**Their fear:** hiring someone they have to supervise closely, or who cannot handle the messy
parts of the job.

**What they are really testing:** can you do the work, do you have judgement, and what are you
like to work with day to day. This is where ownership, prioritisation and how you handle being
wrong get probed.

**Typical questions:** the deep dive on one CV line, the hardest problem you owned, a conflict
with a stakeholder, how you prioritise with too much on the list, a time you were wrong, how
you work with people who do not report to you, why you left or are leaving.

**For Seba specifically:** `master-cv.md` has strong owned-outcome material (the $2M monthly
budget, the UberPro case that changed leadership's mind, the EV fleet renegotiation). A hiring
manager will pick one and push on it hard: what exactly did you do, what did you decide, what
would you do differently. Prepare depth on the two or three most role-relevant bullets, because
a strong bullet with a thin story underneath is worse than not listing it.

**His leadership answers should come from `context/about-me.md`,** which already states his
actual style: raise problems fast, come with a proposed solution, define the what and the when
and leave the how to the team. That is a real position and it interviews well. Use it.

---

## Case, functional or technical (45 to 90 minutes)

**Who:** the hiring manager, a senior peer, or a panel.

**Their fear:** someone who talks strategy fluently but cannot get to a number or a decision.

**Format varies:** live business case, take-home, a presentation of prior work, an analytics or
SQL exercise, a product or ops scenario.

**What they are really testing:** structure, whether you get to the drivers that matter,
whether you can size something roughly and out loud, and what you do when you have to make a
call on incomplete information.

**How to prepare it here:** predict two or three cases specific to this company's real problems,
built from Track A of the research. Not generic frameworks. For each one give the structure, the
levers, the metrics that company would use, the tradeoff that makes it interesting, and where
Seba's own experience connects. See `question-banks.md`.

**The trap:** presenting a framework instead of an answer. Structure first, then commit to a
recommendation and say what would change your mind.

---

## Cross-functional or peer panel (30 to 45 minutes each)

**Who:** people from the functions the role works with. Often the ones who will be blocked by
this person, or blocking them.

**Their fear:** a new colleague who creates work for them, or who cannot be argued with.

**What they are really testing:** collaboration under real disagreement, whether you understand
what their function is measured on, and whether you can be persuaded.

**Typical questions:** a time you disagreed with another team, how you handle a partner team
that will not prioritise your work, how you communicate bad news, what you would need from us
in the first month.

**For Seba specifically:** the cross-functional regulatory initiative with Legal, Policy,
Compliance and Engineering is the strongest single piece of evidence in the bank for this stage.
It should be ready.

---

## Executive or final (30 minutes)

**Who:** a director, VP or founder. Often short, often less structured.

**Their fear:** someone who does not raise the level of the team, or who will not last.

**What they are really testing:** ambition, judgement at altitude, and whether you have a point
of view about their business. Frequently a single hard question and then a conversation.

**Typical questions:** what do you think we are getting wrong, where do you see this market in
three years, what would you do in your first ninety days, why this and not the obvious
alternative, where do you want to be in five years.

**For Seba specifically:** this is where the research pays off, and where his long-term plan
can come up. Handle it honestly and framed around what he would build here, not around the exit.

**The trap:** hedging. Executives read hedging as having no view.

---

## Every stage, without exception

**Questions he asks them.** Prepare three to five per stage, pitched at that person. Recruiter
questions are about process and team. Hiring manager questions are about the work, success in
the role, and what has been hardest. Executive questions are about strategy and bets. At least
one question per stage must be traceable to the research, because that is the question that
proves he did the work.

Never ask a question the careers page answers. Never ask about holiday or remote policy before
an offer, unless the recruiter opened that door.
